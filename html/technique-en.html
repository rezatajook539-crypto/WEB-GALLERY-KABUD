The Technique of Light

How a painter from Delft turned one window, a few rare pigments and endless patience into some of the calmest images in Western art.

Four Secrets of Vermeer's Light

01

Window Light

Many of his interiors are lit by a window on the left. The light falls softly across walls and faces and never feels staged.

02

Natural Ultramarine

He painted with ultramarine made from ground lapis lazuli, one of the costliest pigments of his day, even for shadows and cloth.

03

Points of Light

Tiny dabs of bright paint, often called pointillé, make bread, metal and pearls glitter without sharp outlines.

04

Patient Layers

Thin layers and transparent glazes build depth slowly, which is why his colors seem to glow from within.

A Precious Blue

Ultramarine was ground from lapis lazuli that traveled a long way and cost a fortune. Vermeer used it generously, not only for blue clothes but also in shadows and in places where you would not expect it.

Did He Use a Camera Obscura?

Some historians think Vermeer studied his scenes through a camera obscura, because of the soft, blurry highlights and strong perspective in his work. No document proves it, so the question is still open. Small pinholes at the vanishing point of several paintings show he used a pin and string to set his perspective lines.
